# Process Systems

Use this file for workflow optimization, SOPs, automation, handoffs, quality problems, and internal efficiency.

## Process Diagnosis

Start with the work path:

1. Name the process from trigger to finished output.
2. Identify the customer of the process, internal or external.
3. List each step, owner, input, output, system/tool, queue, and exception path.
4. Mark wait time, rework, duplicate entry, approval loops, and unclear ownership.
5. Choose the bottleneck step or highest rework node first.

Do not automate until unnecessary steps, unclear ownership, and broken input standards are fixed.

## Intervention Ladder

Use the lowest sufficient intervention:

1. Clarify input/output standards.
2. Remove unnecessary steps.
3. Reduce handoffs or batch size.
4. Define owner and escalation threshold.
5. Write or update the SOP.
6. Add checklist or quality gate.
7. Automate repeatable, stable, rules-based work.
8. Redesign the process if the business model or volume has changed.

## Translating Methods Into Actions

- Process boundary thinking becomes: define start trigger, end state, customer, suppliers, inputs, outputs, and quality standard.
- Value-stream thinking becomes: measure wait time versus work time, then remove non-value steps.
- Bottleneck thinking becomes: improve the step that limits total throughput before optimizing other steps.
- Responsibility mapping becomes: decide who owns the output, who contributes, who approves, and who only needs visibility.
- Root-cause analysis becomes: ask why the defect recurs until reaching a controllable process condition.
- Continuous improvement becomes: run small changes, measure, standardize if successful, and repeat.

## Automation Fit

Good automation candidates:

- High volume.
- Stable rules.
- Clear inputs and outputs.
- Low exception rate.
- Measurable time or error reduction.
- Existing owner for maintenance.

Poor automation candidates:

- Process still changing weekly.
- Exceptions require judgment.
- Source data is inconsistent.
- No owner will maintain rules.
- Automation would hide quality problems instead of fixing them.

## Metrics

- Cycle time.
- Lead time.
- Queue time.
- Throughput.
- First-pass yield.
- Rework rate.
- Error rate.
- Cost per transaction.
- SLA attainment.
- Escalation rate.

Pair speed metrics with quality countermetrics.

## Failure Signals

- SOPs exist but people bypass them.
- The process works only when a senior person watches it.
- Teams optimize their own step while total cycle time worsens.
- Automation creates more exception handling.
- Approval steps exist because of trust gaps, not risk.
- Handoffs increase as volume grows.
